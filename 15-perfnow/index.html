2 · Rolling out new protocols

What makes a transition work

“Transition is easiest when changing only one entity still benefits that entity.”

“Transition is easiest when the benefits come to those bearing the costs.” RFC 8170, “Planning for Protocol Adoption and Subsequent Transitions”, Internet Architecture Board, 2017

  • IPv6: in most cases no benefit for the one deploying it, so little adoption.
  • RFC 8170 on IPv6: "there was no incremental benefit to deploying locally".
  • TLS 1.3: the TLS handshake goes from 2 round trips to 1. An immediate benefit, high impact.
  • Bridge to QUIC: also 1 round trip for the whole setup. Huge.

3 · Offer on the server, race on the client

Happy Eyeballs: race the options

IPv4 or IPv6, TCP or QUIC: which one works depends on the user's network. The browser tries them in turn.

  • This is the whole strategy: offer on the server, race on the client.
  • HTTP/3 is faster. Alt-Svc and HTTPS records are an easy way to offer it.
  • The browser races and falls back. Servers only need to offer it.

3 · Offer on the server, race on the client

Why “Happy Eyeballs”?

  • Eyeballs is network operator slang for users: the people looking at the screen.
  • Happy eyeballs: users who never wait on a broken path.
  • 2012: IPv6, with IPv4 as a fallback (RFC 6555, then RFC 8305).
  • Now: HTTP/3 too, with HTTP/2 as a fallback (Happy Eyeballs v3, IETF draft).

4 · Offer on the server

Give Happy Eyeballs both signals

  • Serve HTTP/3 and send an Alt-Svc header.
  • Publish an HTTPS record with alpn="h3":
example.com. 3600 IN HTTPS 1 . alpn="h3,h2"
  • Give it a long lifetime, so browsers have it cached.
