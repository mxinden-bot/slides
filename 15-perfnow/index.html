1 · Protocols evolve

What one round trip costs

  • Distance sets the round trip: 5 to 20 ms within a city, 40 to 80 ms across a country, 150 ms or more across an ocean or on mobile (savearoundtrip.com).
  • 25 ms median: a CDN close to the user. The tail is far longer: 43 ms at P75, 118 ms at P95.
  • What people notice (Nielsen, 1993): 0.1 s feels instant, 1 s keeps the flow of thought, 10 s and attention is gone.
  • A handshake costs several round trips: 3 at P95 is already over 0.1 s.
  • It compounds: the round trip is paid before the HTML arrives, and every image, script and style waits for the HTML. So it delays the whole page, not one request.
  • And it is paid again for every new connection: fonts, images, analytics on other origins.

2 · Rolling out new protocols

IPv6: standardised in 1998

  • IPv6 standardised in 1998. Today about a fifth of Firefox connections, up from 18% a year ago.
  • Firefox data only starts in August 2025.
  • Other perspectives: Google says about half of its users reach it over IPv6. That only counts clients reaching a server that supports IPv6.
  • GitHub still has no IPv6 address.
  • There was a transition path, but it was too complex.
  • The internet routed around it: routing moved up to HTTP. A CDN can serve millions of sites from a single IP address.

2 · Rolling out new protocols

TLS 1.3: standardised in 2018

  • Firefox data only goes back to October 2024 (retention limit).
  • TLS 1.3 here is TCP only: QUIC always uses TLS 1.3, so the real share is higher.
  • TLS 1.3: a client offers it, a server that supports it gains right away, everyone else falls back to TLS 1.2.

2 · Rolling out new protocols

What makes a transition work

“Transition is easiest when changing only one entity still benefits that entity.”

“Transition is easiest when the benefits come to those bearing the costs.” RFC 8170, “Planning for Protocol Adoption and Subsequent Transitions”, Internet Architecture Board, 2017

  • IPv6: in most cases no benefit for the one deploying it, so little adoption.
  • RFC 8170 on IPv6: "there was no incremental benefit to deploying locally".
  • TLS 1.3: the TLS handshake goes from 2 round trips to 1. An immediate benefit, high impact.
  • Bridge to QUIC: also 1 round trip for the whole setup. Huge.

3 · Clients race

Happy Eyeballs: race the options

A, AAAA or HTTPS record  ·  IPv4 or IPv6  ·  TCP + TLS or QUIC  ·  HTTP/1.1, 2 or 3  ·  with or without ECH

  • The browser asks DNS for the HTTPS record, AAAA and A at once.
  • Once the answers are in, it sorts the options: QUIC before TCP, alternating IPv6 and IPv4.
  • It starts the best one, QUIC over IPv6. Usually that connects, and the race is over.
  • Click the diagram to replay.

3 · Clients race

Happy Eyeballs: race the options

A, AAAA or HTTPS record  ·  IPv4 or IPv6  ·  TCP + TLS or QUIC  ·  HTTP/1.1, 2 or 3  ·  with or without ECH

  • QUIC gets no answer, for example antivirus drops UDP.
  • 50 ms later the browser also tries QUIC over IPv4, 150 ms after the first try TCP over IPv6.
  • TCP connects first, the rest is cancelled. TCP over IPv4 never starts.
  • Order and delays as in Firefox: 0, 50, 150, 350 ms after the first attempt.
  • This is the whole strategy: servers advertise, clients race. Servers only need to advertise HTTP/3.

3 · Clients race

Why “Happy Eyeballs”?

  • Eyeballs is network operator slang for users: the people looking at the screen.
  • Eyeballs are latency sensitive, and often on lossy, bad networks.

3 · Clients race

With Happy Eyeballs: more page loads over HTTP/3

  • All versions of the experiment, Firefox 158 to 160: about 100M page loads per branch.
  • Firefox 160 alone gives the same picture: 19.6% to 24.6%.
  • This is the client side: one browser, Firefox, racing better.
  • The client still needs the server: it can only reach HTTP/3 where the server offers it.
  • A server that enables HTTP/3 and publishes an HTTPS record changes it for all its clients at once, so the jump there is likely much larger.

Largest contentful paint (LCP) is faster for most page loads

  • LCP: time from navigation start until the largest image or text block is painted. Here measured at page load (onLoad), not the final LCP.
  • Smaller in percent than FCP, and less time saved at the median: 9 ms for LCP, 16 ms for FCP. Happy Eyeballs saves time in connection setup; LCP adds more after that.
  • The fastest page loads (below about P18) get slightly slower: +1.1% (+3 ms) at P10. Possibly the cost of waiting for the HTTPS record; not explained yet.
  • Firefox 158 and 159. Confidence intervals only for P50 to P99, all exclude zero.

  • Alt-Svc: the browser only learns about HTTP/3 from the response, so the first connection is HTTP/2. HTTP/3 from the next connection on.
  • HTTPS record: DNS already says the server speaks HTTP/3, so the first connection is HTTP/3 too.
  • Do both: the HTTPS record for the first connection, Alt-Svc for browsers that do not look up HTTPS records.

4 · Servers advertise

Enable and advertise

  • Enable HTTP/3, next to HTTP/2.
  • Send Alt-Svc on every response.
  • Publish an HTTPS record with alpn="h3".
  • Enable QUIC 0-RTT.

Servers advertise, clients race.

  • Summary: we are moving to HTTP/3, one round trip less. With 0-RTT the request goes out in the first flight.
  • The problem is the rollout. We don't want to get stuck like IPv6. TLS 1.3 is the better example.
  • For that, servers offer and clients race.
  • Here is what you can do:
  • Q&A: 0-RTT data can be replayed by an attacker.
  • Servers accept it only for safe requests such as GET.
  • HTTP has a 425 Too Early status for the rest (RFC 8470).
